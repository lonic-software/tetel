# TET-30 — reporting prose that moved while its claims stood still

Every observation in this memo was taken against the tetel repository at commit e19a4694bedd1bd2bda91626362f9c2d05f35274, whose subject line is "transplant: select the donor's premises, and answer each one"; every claim below describes that tree. TET-30 asks for one human-owed residue line in `check`: the prose blocks revised since the last grounding pass whose cited claims were not. Not a refusal, no judgement about whether the prose is wrong, and computed rather than authored. This memo settles what the anchor is, which timestamps are compared and across what, what the rule does with a memo nothing has graded, what the reader sees, and what the whole thing cannot see.

*cites: C1*

The answer, up front. Every input this design needs is present in data tetel already ships, so the mechanism is buildable — but the ticket's anchor is wrong in a way that would have made it silent on the very finding it cites. "Since the last grounding pass" walks forward every round, because every pass is briefed on every claim and re-grades unchanged wording, so fix-round prose stops being recent as soon as another round runs. The anchor is therefore the earliest record grading a claim's current wording — the moment that wording first entered proof — and a block is listed when its text postdates the latest such moment among the claims it cites that are in proof. That single change settles the ticket's other ambiguities as side effects: it decides earliest-versus-latest in both quantifiers, it empties the vacuous cases without a special rule, it makes creation and revision the same question, and it stops a grounding pass from blessing prose it never read. Two smaller corrections were forced by attack rather than reached by design, and both are on the other side of the comparison. A block's timestamp is looked up by its text *and* its citation list together, not by its last event: keyed on the event, restoring a paragraph's settled wording could never clear the line, and keyed on text alone, a citation-only revision could silence one in seconds using a claim the fix round had legitimately produced. And the in-proof test is existential, because requiring every citation to be in proof let a single record-less citation suppress a block with nothing red to show for it. What none of this removes is a cross-process clock comparison, which neither digest-shaped alternative examined here can replace, because no evidence record has anywhere to put a digest of prose and the pass that would compute one is required not to read it. That bound is printed once, in a preamble above the entries it qualifies.

## ## What the data can and cannot answer

The ticket's premise that this is computable from data tetel already holds survives contact with the code for every input this design needs, and the shape of that data decides most of the design. Prose and claim logs both ship inside the snapshot beside the memo, every event in them is timestamped, and the citation edge a paragraph carries is a field on the prose event itself. Two details the ticket does not mention narrow what can be built. Neither `prose::load_all` nor `claims::load_all` surfaces a timestamp — both replay their log into a struct that keeps only a revision count — so this check reads the JSONL directly rather than reusing the loaded types the rest of `check` works from. And a prose block's id exists only inside the workspace: `compose::render` writes the block's text and its `*cites:` marker and nothing else, so a residue line that says `P4` names something a reader holding only the memo cannot find. Claim ids do not have that problem — the rendered ledger prints the workspace id verbatim.

*cites: C2*

## ## The anchor: first proof, not last pass

The ticket's phrase "the last grounding pass" is not merely ambiguous, as its own open questions suspected — read literally it is self-defeating, and this is the design's first and largest departure. Every grounding pass is briefed on every claim in the ledger, whether or not it has been graded before, so a pass that finds nothing to change still appends a fresh record against the unchanged wording. The two memos already in this tree show what that produces: one claim carrying ten records on a single unchanged digest, written by three passes across nearly half an hour, and another graded `qualifies` twice by different passes a quarter of an hour apart on text nobody touched. Anchor the comparison at the most recent grading and the anchor walks forward every round; fix-round prose written in round three stops being "since the last pass" the moment round four runs, even though no pass has ever read a word of the prose. On the ticket's own account of it, the trail this residue is named after ran six rounds. So the anchor is the earliest record grading a claim's current wording — the moment that wording first entered proof. A later pass over unchanged text cannot move it, and only a revision of the claim itself, followed by a grading of the new wording, ever does.

*cites: C3*

## ## The rule

With the anchor fixed, the rule almost states itself, and the places where it does not are the places attack put there. A prose block is listed when it is a paragraph rather than a heading, it cites at least one id, at least one cited id resolves to a ledger claim that is in proof, and the block's text timestamp is later than the latest first proof among those in-proof citations. "In proof" is not a new predicate: it is the digest comparison `analyze_ledger` already performs, empty-digest grandfathering included. The block's text timestamp is the earliest event in its history whose text and citation list both equal the current ones. Neither half of that definition was in the first draft of this design and neither is decoration — the next two sections are about what each one closes. The two quantifier choices the ticket left open fall out of the anchor rather than being decided by taste. Per claim, take the earliest in-proof record, because that is when the wording settled. Per block, take the latest of those, because a block is not anchored until its evidence base has settled — taking the earliest instead would list every paragraph whose text merely postdates its first-graded citation, which is nearly all of them. Both memos already in this tree list nothing, and that includes the two real post-pass prose revisions they contain between them, tet29's P4 and tet28's P18. Each was rewritten in a fix round after a pass had run, and each is correctly silent for the same reason: each also cites a claim revised in that same round, whose new wording entered proof only afterwards. The maximum is doing exactly the work it was chosen for. That also means the listing behaviour had to be tested on constructions appended to those logs rather than on a real defect: move a tet29 paragraph past its claim's first proof and touch no claim, and it is listed alone.

*cites: C4*

On the ticket's first open question — creation or revision — the residue is about neither act specifically but about the text's age: the timestamp is derived from the block's events without caring whether the earliest one bearing its current wording was a creation or a rewrite. The exposure is the same in both directions, since a paragraph written fresh under already-graded claims rests on them exactly as ungradedly as a rewritten one, and the ticket's own vector could have arrived either way. Counting creations also cannot inflate the list through paragraphs added to carry a new assertion under a new claim, and the reason is mechanical rather than statistical: a claim not yet in proof cannot anchor a block at all, and once a pass has put it in proof that first proof necessarily postdates a paragraph written before the pass ran. How often a fix-round paragraph takes that shape rather than resting on old claims is a question about how authors behave, and nothing here measures it. What creation-counting does catch is the case worth catching: a new paragraph written under nothing but old, settled claims.

*cites: C5*

What clears a listing is the whole design, and it took two refutations to get right. Start with what is easy: a further grounding pass over unchanged wording appends records but cannot move the earliest one, so no amount of re-grounding clears a block — which is correct, since a grounding pass never reads the prose and could not vouch for it if it did. The first refutation was that reverting a paragraph could not clear it either. Prose history is append-only, so a revert appends a later timestamp; keyed on the last event, restoring the settled wording would have left the listing standing forever. Looking the timestamp up by content rather than by event order fixes that. The second refutation is sharper and came from attacking the fix. Keyed on text alone, an author could leave the text untouched and revise only the citations — an act `prose::revise` does not even inspect, and one the `cite` parameter was added to permit — pointing the paragraph at some claim the fix round had legitimately revised and regraded. The anchor moved forward, the timestamp stayed frozen, the line went out, and the machine partition stayed green. A residue that can be silenced by an act indistinguishable from good authoring is worse than no residue, because it certifies. Keying the timestamp on the text and the citation list together closes it: change either and the timestamp moves with it. A third construction closed with it — an old uncited paragraph handed a citation to an already-settled claim, which under the earlier rule entered the list with a timestamp predating everything and was permanently unlistable. So two things clear a listing: restoring the text and citations the claims settled under, and revising or adding a cited claim and having the new wording graded. The second is precisely what the finding asks for, because the defect being surfaced is prose asserting something no claim carries. The cost is that neither is cheap, and it is worth being blunt about how large it can get. Nothing in the rule bounds the number of blocks it can reach: an author who flow-edits every paragraph of a settled memo will see every paragraph listed, and on a memo shaped like tet29 — twelve blocks, every one a cited paragraph, no headings among them — that is all twelve. So what separates this from the `no scope declared` line that once printed once per claim is not that a full sweep is impossible. It is that a full sweep here would report something the author did, not something tetel is; that it is about this document rather than about the tool; and that two concrete acts clear it.

*cites: C6*

One property is worth settling explicitly, because it is where a rule of this family usually goes wrong: the trigger interprets no English. It is not keyed on deciding from a memo's prose that the memo is in scope. The citation edge is a field the author set with `--cites`, the heading flag is a boolean on the same event, and the ordering is a comparison of integers taken off two logs. Text is consumed in exactly two places, and both are byte comparisons. The in-proof test feeds a claim's proposition into sha256; and finding a block's text timestamp means testing each of its past versions for equality with the current text and citation list, which reads every version of every paragraph as bytes. Nothing anywhere parses a sentence, matches a phrase, or forms a view about what a paragraph is about. What stays undecidable is the verdict rather than the trigger: whether a listed paragraph really does assert something its claims do not carry. That is not narrowed, hinted at or scored here. It goes whole to the reader, which is what the human-owed partition is — the one whose own opening sentence ends "none of this is settled by a passing check".

*cites: C12*

## ## The cross-process comparison, and the digest-shaped alternative that does not exist

Of everything the ticket left open, the doubt about comparing timestamps across workspaces is the one that deserved its suspicion, and it does not dissolve. The claim to be careful with is what exactly is new here. Ordering timestamps written by different processes is not: the mint-window check already places refusals against a mint, and each of those lines was appended by a separate invocation. What is new is that the two sides come from different *workspaces* — one authoring, one grounding — where every existing comparison has stayed inside one, and where every check that could have compared a claim against its evidence used a digest instead. The comparison is sound in unit: both timestamps come from a `now_unix` whose body is token-for-token the same in the only two places the worktree defines it, so the same epoch and the same unit with no timezone term between them. What nothing in either record establishes is that the two processes read the same wall clock. The bound that follows is exact, and the design's position is that it must be printed rather than left implicit: a reported ordering can be wrong only if the grounding process's clock differed from the authoring workspace's by more than the interval between the prose edit and the pass that first put the cited claims in proof. Choosing first proof over last pass is what makes that interval a grounding round rather than seconds in the case this residue exists for — but nothing measured here bounds it from below, and no claim is made that it is always large.

*cites: C7*

So the question becomes whether the digest-shaped alternative the ticket hoped for exists. Two were examined; both fail, and no claim is made that a third could not be found. The sharp one — a record carrying a digest of the prose it vouches for, the exact analogue of `out_of_proof` — cannot be built, for a structural reason rather than an oversight. `out_of_proof` compares digests because a record carries a digest of the very text it graded. No record names or digests a prose block anywhere in its shape, and the brief a pass receives is id, proposition and pin. Putting a prose digest into a record would mean the grounding pass reading the prose it exists to grade blind, which trades the independence the whole loop is built on for a residue line. The weaker one is genuinely constructible and was worked through rather than waved away: `render --out` could append a publication record — each prose block's text digest, each claim's proposition digest and whether anything grades it — and the residue would then be an ordinal comparison between successive publications with no clock in it at all. It is rejected on resolution, and the reason is structural too. A log that samples only when the author renders cannot order two events that fall inside one interval, and a grading followed by a prose edit followed by a single render is one interval. On the ticket's own account, that is the shape the finding took. The clock-free formulation would have been silent on it. That is the trade, stated plainly: the timestamp comparison buys the one ordering an interval-sampled log cannot, and pays for it with a bound it prints.

*cites: C8*

## ## The vacuous case, and what falls outside the rule

The vacuous case needs no special handling, because the in-proof test already does the work — but which test turned out to matter, and attack decided it rather than argument. A memo with no evidence ledger, and a memo halfway through its first draft, list nothing: no cited claim is in proof, so there is no first proof to compare against. The design first required *every* cited claim to be in proof, on the tidy-looking ground that a paragraph resting on an ungrounded claim is already in front of the reader under a line about that claim. It is not the same reader service — `ungrounded: C99` is a line about a claim, not about the paragraph — and worse, it made adding a record-less citation a way to silence any listing at all, with a clean machine partition, since a claim with no records is human-owed rather than out of proof. So the test is existential: one in-proof citation is enough to anchor a block, and a record-less citation contributes nothing to the anchor and cannot suppress anything. Non-overlap survives for what is still skipped — a block every one of whose citations is ungrounded or out of proof is already reported, under `ungrounded` or under a machine failure. Two populations remain outside the rule entirely. Headings, which cite nothing by construction, and paragraphs that cite nothing by choice. The second is a real gap: an uncited paragraph is the one place a false assertion can sit with no citation edge to compare it against, and that belongs in the printed non-coverage list rather than in a footnote here.

*cites: C9*

## ## What the reader sees

The last open question asks whether this is a line or a section, and the answer is neither exactly: it is bullets in the flat human-owed list, one per listed block, with a single preamble bullet ahead of them. There are no sub-headings in that partition today, so a section would be the only one and would announce by its shape that it outranks everything beside it. The preamble carries the one thing constant across the entries — that the ordering rests on two clocks nothing here can prove are the same — and it is printed once rather than per bullet for the reason this crate already recorded when it collapsed `no scope declared` to a single document-level line: repeating a constant per row buries the findings that are actually about the document. Each entry names the block's workspace id and the line in this document where its text begins, because a block's id is never written into the rendered output and an id alone points at something the reader cannot find; that mapping comes from one new function in `compose`, consumed by `render`, so the block-to-line correspondence is described once. The entry then names each cited claim with its first-proof timestamp and the pass that wrote it, and the block's own timestamp — the inputs to the comparison, printed so a reader can disagree with the conclusion rather than having to trust it.

## ## Implementation surface, and what it deliberately does not catch

Everything the implementer touches is declared in the modification-target section above, and there are six symbols: a field on `Findings`; a new `prose_after_proof`; the call to it inside `check_file`'s existing snapshot block, whose other occupants are the note-versus-extent check, the tree-state findings, the census and premise re-verifications and the mint windows; a `block_offsets` in `compose` that `render` consumes; the print loop and one added clause in the human-owed scope sentence; and the new `NON_COVERAGE` entries. That block is where this finding's neighbours live; it is not everything in `check` that touches a snapshot, since the drift check and the authoring-identity read reach into one from outside it. One instruction in the list is worth stating precisely, because getting it wrong fails silently. The check has two inputs: the snapshot's `prose.jsonl`, read directly because the loader discards timestamps, and the claims and evidence `check_file` already holds. Those claims must be the ones `check_file` imports from the rendered document, never the snapshot's `claims.jsonl` — every digest on record was computed over a ledger-derived proposition, and the two are not the same string, since rendering replaces newlines with spaces and importing trims every cell. Implemented against the workspace log, the check would find no matching digest for such a claim, conclude it is not in proof, and drop every block citing it, with nothing red to say so. `machine_check_failed` is untouched, which is what makes the line human-owed by construction rather than by good intentions. Nothing new ships in the snapshot; no schema changes; nothing is executed and no file the document names is opened, so `check`'s standing guarantees hold unchanged. Without a snapshot the list is simply not computed, in silence, exactly as the note-versus-extent check and the mint windows are not. Five things go into the printed non-coverage list. False prose that ships alongside a claim revision, which the ticket concedes and this design does not fix. A paragraph that cites nothing. A citation added to a claim not yet in proof, which cannot be listed, because by the time any pass proves that claim its first proof postdates the edit. Whether the two clocks agree. And whether the prose is wrong, which nothing here judges and nothing here should.

*cites: C11*

One measurement made in passing deserves recording, because the next ticket in this family will meet it. Declaring these six targets required six whole-worktree searches, and a census captures its entire output into a fact that ships in the snapshot. This repository's previous two snapshots hold whole file dumps on single JSON lines — tet29's longest is 57249 bytes, tet28's 34477 — so a search for a symbol this crate discusses re-captures those dumps. What such a search costs turns out to be a property of the instrument and the moment rather than of the symbol, in two separate ways. Scope: `render` matches 486 lines and 373271 bytes of this tree in the state the census was taken in, or 447 and 311672 with `.git/` and `target/` excluded as well; `NON_COVERAGE` 35 and 95637, `Findings` 23 and 57418, `check_file` 5 and 285, and the two symbols that do not exist yet nothing at all. Those numbers hold still because the trees they range over are frozen. Time: the same counts taken with this memo's own artifacts included do not hold still at all. `render` measured 529 lines and 1136689 bytes at one point in this memo's review and 550 and 1202134 later, with no source change in between — what grew was this memo's evidence log, now 59 records and 164392 bytes, and its snapshot, whose longest fact line is the 574898-byte capture of the `render` census itself. Grading a claim about census cost enlarges the thing the claim is about, which is the compounding thesis demonstrating itself. It is also the one part of this section no independent pass can confirm, along with the count of six searches: both range over this memo's own logs and snapshot, which a pass grading this memo must not open, so they are recorded here expecting to stay ungraded. What any pass can check, and what survives every way of scoping the count, is the ordering and the direction: censusing a symbol this crate talks about costs orders of magnitude more than censusing one it does not, and every memo added under `docs/design/` enlarges the next memo's census of anything it discusses.
