# A mint-time verifier: warn when what an author wrote disagrees with what the tool captured

This design proposes that `fact`, `claim` and `prose` compare the text the author just wrote against the evidence the tool already holds, and say so in the tool result when the two disagree. The comparison is a model call, not a string operation, and it is triggered at the moment of writing rather than in a later grounding round.

The whole proposal stands on one inherited argument, and on getting the inheritance right. `scope.rs` exists because the project's flagship `Domain ⊆ Extent` check was abandoned as vacuous: with one author supplying both sides, the sides agreed byte-for-byte about four times in five, and independence rather than capture was what moved that number. What rescued the comparison was making one side structural — an extent is captured by `look`/`run`, and there is deliberately no flag to type one in, while a note is authored. That construction has two halves, and only the first is free here. The captured side cannot be typed: an author can no more type a cited fact's captured output than they can type an extent. But the captured side is also complete — `look` and `run` record what was actually opened — and for a claim the captured side would be whatever the author chose to cite. An overreaching proposition could then be made to agree with its evidence by citing only the facts that agree with it, and the verifier would see no disagreement because the disagreeing evidence never entered the comparison. That is the author's diligence doing the work again, which is the failure the whole construction exists to avoid.

tetel already knows this choice is unreliable, which is why creating a claim hands back an overlap report: every fact touching the same files that the author did not cite. So the comparison is defined over the cited facts together with that overlap set. Both are on disk at claim time and neither costs a new mechanism — inside `create`, the overlap report is computed at `src/claims.rs:136` and the claim event is appended at `:140`, four lines and two statements later, from a load of every fact in the workspace, and the set is handed back in the same outcome. With that union as the captured side, neither half can be typed and neither can be narrowed by selection, and the independence transfers intact. That is what makes a semantic version of the comparison worth building at all.

*cites: C1*

It is also aimed at a gap the existing check names for itself. `scope.rs` reports that of the two overreaching facts in the memo that motivated it, it catches one — the one that named a file and line range it never opened. The other asserted that a client "mirrors the server's" walk while its extent covered only the client, and is missed because it names no location at all: invisible, in the module's own words, to any check of that shape. The one refinement tried and rejected failed for the same reason recorded in the same place — naming a symbol the code you read calls is normal, concluding about what that symbol does is the failure, and those two are not distinguishable by string matching. A check that compares meaning is the check that residual describes. This design is `scope.rs` for meaning rather than for location, and it inherits `scope.rs`'s posture along with its argument: human-owed, never a refusal.

*cites: C2*

## ## The surface

The mint results already carry two findings of this family, and they set the register. `fact` returns `attention`, a top-level array whose entries say a note names a location the extent does not cover; `claim` returns `overlap`, the ids and shared extent keys of facts the author looked at and did not cite. Both are shaped as fields rather than sentences on the stated ground that the caller is a program, and a field it can branch on beats a string it has to notice. The verifier's finding belongs in the same place for the same reason — and, following the same doc comment, for the reason that only the author, near the moment of writing, can still cheaply tell context from conclusion.

It should not, however, be a third array. `attention` and `overlap` are computed per call out of workspace state that is already on disk, so for them an empty array and an absent one carry the same information: nothing was found. A model call can be switched off, be refused for want of a key, fail in transport, time out, or come back unreadable, and in every one of those states "found nothing" is a different fact from "did not look". A verification also outlives the reply that triggered it, so the result carrying the mint cannot report the verification's outcome at all. Only an object holds both halves. `verify` therefore has a mandatory `status`: the reply that triggers a verification says `off`, `unauthorized` or `queued`, each decidable without a call, and the reply that delivers one says `ok`, `unavailable`, `timeout` or `unparsable`, naming the mint its findings concern, since that is no longer the id sitting beside them. `findings` is meaningful only under `ok`. A caller that branches on `verify.status` cannot mistake a silent outage, a disabled feature or a call still in flight for a clean bill.

*cites: C3*

The register travels with the finding rather than only in the tool description. The precedent carries it in both places, but not in the same words, and the split is the argument rather than an embarrassment to it. In the payload, each `attention` entry carries its own `guidance` string, built beside the finding by `scope::advice` and hedged in that function's own terms — "Fine if that's context. If it's a conclusion about {}, you have not read it here". The crisper sentence, "It is not an error — read it and decide", is not `attention`'s at all: it belongs to the `claim` tool's description and is about the overlap report. So the sharpest statement of the register sits on the surface a caller loads once and may never read again, while the finding that needs it arrives alone — which is the reason to put a register string in the finding. What the argument cannot lean on is a claim that the guidance rides along inside one builder for free. It does not: `fact_result` builds the two `fact` results, while `claim` and `prose` results are assembled by inline literals at their own sites, and the default-on verb is one of the two `fact_result` never touches. The drift this raises is real and has to be answered directly, so `verify` is added at each of the three sites and the guidance text, the status vocabulary and the non-determinism marker are all produced by the shared `verify_block` helper rather than restated per verb. That is the discipline `scope::advice` already enforces for `attention`, one definition reaching both the MCP payload at `src/mcp.rs:185` and the CLI at `src/main.rs:650` and `:657`, so two surfaces cannot disagree about one finding.

*cites: C19*

One field in that object is not optional and has no counterpart in the precedent. `attention` and `overlap` recompute to the same answer every time, because their inputs are files on disk. This check does not: the eval measured, on identical input at temperature 0, reasoning length swinging between 516 and 2000 tokens, with the draws that reach the token ceiling returning an empty response body. Two identical mints can therefore warn differently, or warn once and stay silent the next time. A reader who sees a third finding sitting beside two deterministic ones will assume parity unless told otherwise, so the object names the model that produced it and carries `deterministic: false` in every response, including the silent ones.

*cites: C4*

## ## What the eval settled, and what it did not

The measurement in `scripts/verifier-eval/` decides the shape of the check and deliberately decides nothing about its worth. On fifteen cases run three times, the one-call direct comparison caught thirty-two of thirty-three planted defects and left all twelve sound claims alone for six tenths of a cent; the three-call extract-then-judge pipeline managed twenty-eight of thirty-two and seven of twelve for nearly nine times the money. Better on both axes at a ninth of the cost is not a close call, and the hypothesis that extracting first and comparing second would beat a single call did not survive contact with the numbers. The direct comparison is therefore the default.

What the same README says about itself is the more important half. The cases are synthetic, none taken from a real memo; the set is small and was written by the same process that then measured against it; one case was discovered to be mislabeled only because the model disagreed with it, which is a warning about the remaining fourteen as much as a reassurance about the model. And the section closes by naming the thing it does not measure at all — whether warning an author earlier removes a grounding round, which is the entire cost argument for the feature. That sentence is the reason nothing here was built before a retrodiction test on real memos, and the last section of this document records what that test returned: the cost argument named in this paragraph is the one thing it did not survive.

*cites: C5*

One correction to the paragraph above, made after the retrodiction rather than before it. The eval settled that the direct comparison beat the pipeline, and this document concluded that direct was therefore the default — but every number the gate section rests on was produced in a different configuration, one that classifies each assertion as current, proposed or argument before checking only the current ones. It would be too strong to say the one-call comparison was never tried on a real claim: it was, over this very corpus, with the eval's own prompt. What is true is narrower and still decisive. That arm is not the one shipped as `direct`, and no figure it produced was carried into the gate or into any threshold declared here. So the choice is between a mechanism whose numbers are on the page and one whose numbers are not, and picking the second because it is cheaper is the substitution this document refuses two paragraphs earlier when it declines to lay the fourteen-case figures beside the fifteen-case columns. Nor is the saving what "one call instead of two" sounds like. The call dropped carries the claim text alone; the call kept carries the bounded evidence, so the asymmetry predicts less than a halving. What the disk says depends on which one-call arm is taken as the analogue, and the spread is wide enough that the choice has to be made in the open: the arms that report disagreements, which is the shape a finding has to have, cost 0.63 and 0.65 of the split mean, while the arms that grade a verdict the product never emits cost 0.26 and 0.36. On the nearer reading that is about a third off, for a mechanism nothing on the page grades. The default is the configuration that was measured; direct is one setting away for anyone content with that trade.

*cites: C24*

A pipeline is nevertheless kept as a selectable mode, and it is not the one the previous paragraph describes being beaten. Those are two harnesses, and an earlier drafting of this section treated them as one. The README's own table of shapes separates them by the property at issue: row B runs two calls and has Python compare two extracted records, its re-derivable-without-a-model cell reading yes; row C runs three calls and hands those same two records to a model to judge, its cell reading no. It is C, the three-call one, that the direct comparison beat in the fifteen-case columns for nearly nine times the money. It is B, the two-call one, that is worth keeping — and the reason has nothing to do with its score. Its accuracy was measured on an earlier fourteen-case set and cannot be laid beside the fifteen-case columns at all. What it has is a property the winner cannot acquire: its findings are re-derivable by anyone holding the two extracted records, with no model in the loop, while the direct comparison's are re-derivable only by asking a model again. That the property and the identity travel together is the whole point, since re-derivability is exactly what tells the two rows apart — a mode kept for being checkable without a model cannot be the mode whose decision a model makes. For an author who needs a finding to survive being handed to someone without an API key, that is worth two calls and about seven times the money — but it is a deliberate selection, never the default.

## ## Configuration, and the key that never lands in it

`config.rs` sets an admission test before it offers a registry: every setting in that file must be visible in the output it affects, and a setting that cannot be shown that way should not be added. The grounding floor passes because it is printed beside the owed list it produced. Five keys are wanted here — `verify.enabled`, `verify.model`, `verify.approach`, `verify.timeout_ms` and `verify.verbs` — and it is worth being exact about which of them an echo of status, model and non-determinism would actually cover. `verify.enabled` shows up as a status of `off` and `verify.model` by name; `verify.timeout_ms` would surface only in the one state that trips it, `verify.verbs` only by inference from an absence, and `verify.approach` — the choice between two materially different mechanisms — nowhere at all, so a reader could not tell which of them produced the finding in front of them. Under the rule as the module states it, that is a disqualification and not a rough edge. The answer is to widen the echo rather than to keep a key in defiance of the sentence quoted to justify it: `verify_block` writes the effective value of all five into every `verify` object it builds — status, model, approach, timeout and the verb list — beside `deterministic: false`, including on the calls where the setting made nothing happen. Each key is then one entry in the existing static registry with its own `accepts` rule, and each is on the page of every response it affects. They need neither a new scope nor a new file. The absent project scope stays absent for the reason the module already gives: such a file would have to be written inside the repository under design, where the workspace module refuses to write at all.

*cites: C8*

The API key is not among them and must come from the environment, because config files are shared, committed and pasted into issues. Most of that rule is already enforced without new code: there is no `verify.api_key` entry, `set` resolves every key through the registry and refuses what it does not find, and the refusal lists the known keys so an author who tried is told what they could have written instead. One gap remains, and it is at the value level rather than the key level — `verify.model` is a string key and would cheerfully accept a credential pasted into it. The strict-when-set half of the module's asymmetry is exactly the right hook: a new `accepts` rule that refuses a credential-shaped value at write time. This does not breach the rule that refusals are format-level, since `set` already refuses on value shape by construction, and a refusal at `config set` can never suppress a mint.

## ## The crate property this breaks

`lib.rs` pins two properties across the whole crate, and this feature makes one of them false the day it lands: there are no network calls anywhere, which the dependency set enforces rather than the prose. The parenthesis is the part that matters. Once an HTTP client is linked the dependency set enforces nothing, and a softened version of the same sentence would be prose impersonating an invariant. The module has already been through this once and says so in its preamble — a single sentence about the whole crate was wrong for months, which is why the write list is stated per command — so the replacement takes the form the file already prefers, and it has to cover all fifteen commands the file lists or it reintroduces the same defect through the enumeration instead of the generalisation. Three reach: `fact`, `claim` and `prose` may make one outbound call to the configured provider, and only when the feature is enabled with a key present in the environment. Eleven make none: `check`, `brief`, `query`, `review`, `workspaces`, `look`, `target`, `transplant`, `render`, `record` and `config`. `run` gets its own line, because neither list is true of it. The crate makes no call of its own for `run`; `run` spawns the command the author typed, in the author's own session, and that process can reach the network exactly as anything else the author runs in a terminal can. The categorical sentence never had to say this, being about the crate's own calls; a per-command row does. The second pinned property, that nothing here ever executes a command named by a document, survives intact — `run` executes what the author types, never what a memo contains — and it is the reason `check` is on the eleven and not the three.

## ## Which partition it belongs to

Neither. `report.rs` holds two arrays of canonical category names, one per partition, and they exist as a single enumerated source because three hand-maintained copies of the same lists had already drifted apart — the module's scope strings and the `check` tool description are both generated from them. So the arrays are not a taxonomy of findings in general; they are a statement of what `check` covers. This check does not run inside `check`, does not enter the record, the memo, the snapshot or the evidence ledger, and does not repeat: the same input can produce a different answer, so a reader holding the document could not recompute it even in principle. Adding it to either array would make a scope string promise coverage `check` does not have, which is the exact failure the arrays were introduced to end. The warning lives in the mint result and nowhere else.

## ## Where findings persist

A workspace already keeps a log of things that happened but are not records — `refusals.log` — and a sibling `verify.log` is the natural home for findings an author scrolled past. But `refusals.log` is exactly the wrong sibling to copy in one respect: it is an entry in `SNAPSHOT_FILES`, so it is shipped into `<memo>.tetel/` on every render. A log of model output shipped that way would put non-reproducible text inside the snapshot, which is the one place this design has promised never to reach. What rescues the idea is the shape of the list rather than the manners around it: `SNAPSHOT_FILES` is an enumeration of ten names, and `write` walks exactly that array and copies exactly those names, so a workspace file whose name is not in it cannot be copied at all. The array says as much about itself — enumerated rather than copied wholesale, so that a future workspace file is a deliberate decision to ship or withhold rather than an accident of `cp -r`. It would be wrong to lean on the entries' comments for this: only four of the ten carry one, and of the six that do not, only `pending.json` is argued for in the array's doc comment. The comment is a courtesy; the enumeration is the mechanism. So `verify.log` stays out of the snapshot by construction, needs no mechanism to keep it there, and shipping it later would take a deliberate line in that array.

## ## What is compared, and what it costs

Mints are instant today, and the eval's calls were not. The cost is easy to underestimate because the unit paying it is the tool call rather than the record: in the largest memo on disk, the append-only logs hold two hundred and seventy-seven lines across facts, claims and prose, of which a hundred and fourteen claim lines resolve to thirty-nine distinct claims. Two thirds of claim traffic is revision. Verifying every line of a run like that is tens of minutes of added wall-clock against a few cents of spend, so the design is shaped by the clock and not by the bill.

Committing the mint before the call is necessary and not sufficient, because a mint result is one object. `fact_result` carries `attention`, `folded` and `refused_since_previous_fact` in the same payload the verifier would join; the claim result carries `overlap`. All of those are deterministic findings that arrive with certainty today only because a mint is instant, and putting a provider call in front of the reply makes every one of them contingent on the provider: a client whose own request timeout is shorter than `verify.timeout_ms`, or an author who interrupts, gets the record committed and a note-outside-extent warning thrown away that no model was ever involved in producing.

So nothing in the reply path waits. The mint result returns immediately, carrying only what can be decided without a call. The comparison runs after the reply, and its finding is delivered on the author's next authoring call in that workspace, and appended to `verify.log`. This is not a new idea in this codebase: a mint already reports the refusals recorded in the window before it, through one helper shared by the CLI and the MCP surface so the two cannot drift on which refusals a mint reports. The remaining rules follow. `verify.timeout_ms` bounds a mint's verification end to end rather than a single HTTP attempt, and since it no longer sits in front of a reply it can be generous. A revision that does not change the text being compared makes no call at all, which is where most of the volume lives. The price is that a warning lands one tool call after the writing it concerns; what is bought is the removal of the entire latency budget from the author's critical path, and one tool call later is still many rounds earlier than a grounding pass.

*cites: C12*

Which of the three verbs is checked by default is the one place this design has to correct the eval's own headline before it can use it. It is tempting to say `claim` is on because `claim` is the verb that was measured. That is false. The fifteen-case set is two files: twelve in-scope cases from `cases.py`, and three from `extra_cases.py`, which opens by saying its cases are aimed at the note-versus-extent check specifically — the `fact` comparison — and four harnesses in the directory build their run the same way, as the in-scope cases plus those three, the same line verbatim in `direct_eval.py`, `judge_eval.py`, `extract_eval.py` and `extract2.py`. Two of those four are the ones behind the reported columns, not all four: the README's result table for the fifteen-case set has exactly two rows, A for `direct_eval.py` and C for `judge_eval.py`. The other two share the assembly without standing behind those columns — `extract2.py`'s numbers are the earlier fourteen-case figures printed in prose beside the table, which this memo has already set aside as not comparable, and `extract_eval.py` is the file table's "B's first draft". What the four have in common is how they build their case list, which is what makes the composition of the reported runs readable off the source. That is a statement about those four and not about the directory: `run_eval.py`, which the README's own file table calls the original verdict harness, imports `CASES` alone, filters by `--only` and by an optional `--scope` flag that defaults to all, and never touches `EXTRA_CASES` — it does not assemble its run this way and is not one of the four. Within the four, and so within both of the runs the headline came from, two of each run's eleven defective cases and one of its four sound ones are therefore `fact` cases, and the thirty-two of thirty-three and twelve of twelve are sums over both verbs with no per-verb column anywhere in the output. So `fact` has numbers, `claim`'s cannot be separated from them, and the honest reading is that the eval licenses no per-verb accuracy statement at all — a reason for more caution about those columns, not for preferring one verb over another. The default set is decided on coverage and volume instead. `claim` is the comparison nothing in the crate performs today: `attention` reads a note against its own extent, and `overlap` is deliberately ids and shared extent keys rather than notes, so no existing check reads a proposition against the content of the evidence cited for it. `fact` is the one verb already half covered, deterministically and for free, by `scope` — and this design declines to say how large the remaining semantic residue is, because nothing measures it: `scope.rs` reports catching one of the two overreaches that motivated it, which is parity rather than a smaller half, and `extra_cases.py` describes the deterministic check as catching a note that names an unopened file only when that file's name happens not to appear anywhere in the extent string. `fact` stays off because half its work is already done and the rest is unsized, and it is the first verb to turn on when someone sizes it. `prose` stays off for a plainer reason: it is the highest-volume verb of the three in the memo measured, at a hundred and thirty log lines against a hundred and fourteen for claims and thirty-three for facts, and prose-against-propositions is a comparison neither case file contains. Defaulting it on would spend the largest share of the budget on the least-evidenced comparison.

## ## The modification surface

Five symbols are declared as modification targets, and each was censused across the whole worktree before being named here. The first draft of this section named three, and the census that corrected it is the reason the number moved. `fact_result` is defined once and called twice in production, from the minted and the revised arms of the `fact` tool — but it builds only `fact` results. A `claim` result is assembled inline, in the three `ClaimOutcome` match arms at `src/mcp.rs:700`, `709` and `710`, where `fact_result` does not appear at all; `prose` is assembled the same way in the three `ProseOutcome` arms at `821`, `822` and `823`. Since `claim` is the verb this design turns on by default, a `verify` key added to `fact_result` alone would reach everything except the path that ships enabled. So `ClaimOutcome` and `ProseOutcome` join the list, and with them a `verify_block` helper — censused at zero occurrences, so the name is free — that every site calls, so that the status vocabulary, the model name, the `deterministic: false` marker and the guidance string cannot drift between verbs. The config half is genuinely self-contained: `known_keys` occurs twice in the entire worktree, both inside the config module, and `Accepts` six times, likewise all inside it with one variant in use, so the work is new registry entries, new `Accepts` variants, and matching arms in the validator and in the message that tells an author what they could have written instead. The same censuses show the CLI in `src/main.rs` matching on both outcome enums; it is not modified, because the finding is a field in an MCP result and the CLI surfaces stay as they are.

## ## The degradation contract

Every terminal state of the provider call maps to exactly one `status`, and the mapping has to be total or it reproduces the defect it exists to prevent. Disabled by configuration is `off`. No key in the environment is `unauthorized`. A transport failure or a non-2xx reply is `unavailable`. Expiry of the budget is `timeout`. A truncated draw is the case the eval already met — reaching the token ceiling comes back with a body of length zero, which is trivially easy to treat as a clean result — and it is `unavailable`, never `ok`. The state an earlier drafting of this contract missed is the one that is hardest to see: a perfectly good 2xx reply, non-empty and non-truncated, whose content is not a usable answer. The harness this design borrows its contract from meets that state in three distinct shapes — no brace-delimited substring in the body at all, a substring that fails `json.loads`, and a decoded object whose verdict is outside the three permitted values — and none of them is a transport failure, a non-2xx, an empty body or a truncation. All three are `unparsable`. `ok` is reachable only from a decoded verdict in the fixed vocabulary, which is what keeps an unreadable answer from arriving as a clean bill with an empty `findings` array. That is precisely the mistake the eval's own harness made in its first form — an errored row recorded as an unflagged one, which reads as a miss on a defective case and a correct silence on a sound one, wrong in both directions at once — and the fix there was to give errors their own column entering no denominator. `status` is the same discipline. The budget's unit is stated with it: `verify.timeout_ms` bounds one mint's verification end to end, across every retry, because the harness's own call retries up to three times with a tripled cap, and a per-attempt bound would quietly license three times the declared spend. And since the call runs after the mint result has already gone back, every state here is reported on a later call and none of them can fail a mint, delay a reply or move an exit code.

*cites: C15, C3*

A finding's contents matter more than its container, because the author receiving it is usually an agent and an agent acts on warnings. A false positive that arrives as a verdict gets a correct claim "fixed". The eval has a measured instance of exactly this: the pipeline's false positives were all one failure mode, and the recorded one reported that the evidence contained no pin name or binding against evidence whose lines 425-436 are the pin computation. Stated as a verdict, that can only be accepted or ignored. Stated as a quotation — this clause, judged against these captured lines — its wrongness is visible in a single step to anyone holding the memo. So an entry names the clause it is judging, the fact id, and the span of captured output it was compared against, all quoted, and carries no confidence score. A number invites deference; a quotation invites checking, and checking is the only response that is safe when the check is wrong.

*cites: C16*

Which raises the question the previous paragraph does not answer: nothing so far makes the quotation true. A quoted span in a finding is a string a model produced, recovered from free text by a regular expression and a JSON parse, and a fabricated quotation is strictly worse than a bare verdict — it sends a recipient to check against text that does not exist, so the one response that is safe when the check is wrong is the response it defeats. This repository already owns the fix and applies it to exactly this kind of authored claim about captured evidence. `Fact::quotes` decides whether a string is a verbatim substring of a single observation's captured output: byte for byte, with nothing normalised, and single rather than joined so that a span straddling two observations cannot be assembled out of text that was never contiguous in anything anyone looked at. It is what makes `transplant` refuse a premise that is not the donor's own words, and it is a plain substring search — no model, no network, no knowledge of the language it is reading. So `verify_block` puts every finding's quoted span through it before that finding enters the payload. A span it accepts ships as a quotation. A span it rejects is dropped, and its finding is downgraded to one that carries no evidence quotation and says so. Nothing is refused and no mint is affected; the check runs on the model's output rather than the author's, and all it withholds is the authority a quotation carries from text that did not come from the captured record.

*cites: C20*

## ## The gate

Nothing described above was to be built before the retrodiction test, and the corpus for it was already on disk. Seven memos each carry an evidence log beside a snapshot. The claim logs record every Create and Revise with a timestamp and the wording carried at that moment, so a claim's text as it stood at first render can be reconstructed exactly — whenever the claim existed at first render at all. The evidence logs are in-toto statements whose subject is a claim id and whose predicate carries a verdict, and across all seven there are 786 of them: 578 supports, 185 qualifies, 23 refutes. Those are row counts, and rows are the wrong unit for this test. The verifier runs once per claim, over one wording, and returns at most one flag; resolved onto claims, the same 786 rows cover 143 distinct claims, of which only 125 were created at or before their memo's first evidence row. The other eighteen have no first-render wording to feed the verifier — five of them among the fourteen claims that were ever refuted — and they leave every denominator rather than counting as misses, which is the discipline the eval imposed on its own errored rows for the same reason. What remains is three populations the test can iterate: 62 claims carrying supports and nothing else, 9 carrying at least one refutes, 60 carrying at least one qualifies. For each, two questions: did the verifier flag that first-render wording, and did the flag name the same defect a later pass named.

*cites: C17*

Two outcomes kill the feature, and both are declared here rather than after the run. If 7 or more of the 62 claims that carry supports and nothing else are flagged at their first-render wording, it is a steering hazard: each of those flags is an invitation to an agent to damage a claim that was already sound. If 2 or fewer of the 9 refuted claims that have a first-render wording are surfaced with a finding naming the defect a later pass named, the verifier does not catch what forces a revision round, and the cost argument collapses on the only corpus capable of testing it. Both are counts of claims over counts of claims, which an earlier drafting of this gate was not, and the difference is not cosmetic: taken against the 578 supports rows, flagging every sound claim in the corpus scores 10.7 per cent against a ten per cent line, and an eighty-one per cent false-positive rate — fifty flags — passes at 8.7 per cent. Nor is that an artefact of which row count is used: over the 510 supports rows that belong to claims with a first-render wording the same two numerators give 12.2 and 9.8 per cent, and the eighty-one per cent rate still passes. A gate that cannot fail is worse than no gate. The recall side deserves less confidence than the false-positive side, because nine is a small denominator and a single claim moves it eleven points: clearing that line is the absence of a reason to stop, not a reason to build. A disappointing showing on the 60 claims carrying a qualifies is not a kill either, because a qualification is frequently a legitimate narrowing of scope rather than a defect the author should have avoided, and treating that bucket as the target would reward a verifier that flags everything.

*cites: C18*

## What the gate returned

The test ran over all 125 claims, three draws each, in the configuration six harness iterations settled on: assertions classified before they are checked, the whole claim held in view while each assertion is checked, and the captured side taken as the cited facts together with the overlap set. It returned a split verdict, not a pass.
